Frieren: Through the Lens of Perception

Cristina Elisabet Garcia Wood,

November 18, 2026.

Frieren: Beyond Journey's End is a manga and animated series that follows the lives of a group of heroes after the completion of their quest to defeat the Demon King. Throughout the series, we explore several key interpersonal communication concepts, such as the perception process, perception checking, the Johari window, social comparison, and self-concept. And most significantly: the manner in which our perception of time influences how we understand and value our relationships with other people.

Artifact

This story begins after the end of the heroes' ten-year journey to defeat the Demon King. Frieren is an elf who has lived for over several thousand years; her non-elf companions possess significantly shorter lifespans of less than one hundred years. According to Carstensen et al. (1999), in Taking Time Seriously: A Theory of Socioemotional Selectivity, "When time is perceived as open-ended, knowledge-related goals are prioritized. In contrast, when time is perceived as limited, emotional goals assume primacy" (p. 165). This proved to be a continuous pattern within the series: everyone seemed to know more about her, than she knew about them. For her, the priority was: magic and collecting grimoires, whereas for her companions, it was the relationships and experiences that they had built together. For Frieren, ten years was not long and insignificant, but for others, it was "the journey of a lifetime" (Yamanaka et al., 2023–present). They experienced the same journey together, though the significance they asigned this span of time was different.

This pattern continues; however, as Episode One progresses, Frieren's schemata begins to adjust. During her travel companion Himmel's funeral, she notes, "I knew human lives were short; why didn't I try to get to know him better?" (Yamanaka et al., 2023–present). Initially, it hadn't meant very much to her that they had spent ten years together, as this represented only a brief moment in her long life. Her companions, on the other hand, understood that Frieren lived much longer than they did; they accepted that this shaped her worldview and experience of life. We refer to these structures of understanding as our schemata, which our textbook compares to "databases of stored, related information that we use to interpret new experiences" (Felt & Packer, 2023, "Interpreting Information" section, para. 1). Frieren had always known that human lives were short, but she hadn't fully considered what this meant for the relationships she shared with them. Himmel's death challenged her existing database of understanding, leading to experiences she had previously regarded as insignificant, to hold new meanings.

How can we better understand other people's perspectives before they leave our lives? In our course, we learn about perception checking, a process that involves observing someone's behavior, presenting two or more plausible interpretations, seeking clarification, and adjusting our understanding based on their response (Felt & Packer, 2023, Section 2.4). This can aid in our understanding of others, and when performed on us, as the recipients, it can also help bring to light emotions or parts of ourselves that we may not have entirely realized yet. This brings us to the Johari window (Felt & Packer, 2023, Section 3.4), a model that organizes information about ourselves into four areas: what we and others both know, what we know but others don't, what others know but we don't, as well as what remains unknown to both. Organizing information about ourselves and others into this window can help us recognize the limits of our understanding, whilst also accepting that others may not necessarily know everything about themselves.

In Episode One, Himmel exhibits patience for Frieren, acknowledging that she may not understand yet how short human lives are when she suggests that they meet again in fifty years to hike to a better viewing spot for the semicentennial meteor shower. In the episode, he notes that he is trying to enjoy the moment, as she is mainly focused on the logistics of the viewing-point (Yamanaka et al., 2023–present). For Frieren, fifty years isn't a particularly long time, but to Himmel, it represents a significant portion of his life. I think Frieren's understanding of the importance of her human companions can be interpreted through the blind area of the Johari window. Her companions seemed to recognize that their relationships meant something to her, even if she hadn't entirely recognized those feelings herself. It took time, and eventually Himmel's death, for her schemata to adjust. The emotional significance of these relationships had not yet become salient to her, as they did not seem immediately relevant to her needs or interests (Felt & Packer, 2023, Section 2.1). Her priorities had always been staying alive and learning magic; however, time brought new experiences, and she began to discover elements of life that she didn’t previously know were important to her.

Social Comparison Theory

As the series continues, Frieren masks the quantity of mana she emits around her. Her mentor, Flamme, describes displaying mana as being similar to the clothing people wear to show their status and signal their roles in society (Yamanaka et al., 2023–present). Mana functions like a form of currency in the world of magic, as the amount someone possesses can influence how others perceive their abilities. By hiding her mana, Frieren encourages other magical creatures to underestimate her, allowing her to approach or avoid her opponents without being challenged or revealing her true strength. I found this interesting with regard to social comparison theory (Felt & Packer, 2023, Section 3.4), which encompasses how we evaluate ourselves in comparison to others. Our textbook notes that social comparison can bring about "relational insecurity," particularly when people perceive themselves as inferior to others whom they regard as more secure or superior (Felt & Packer, 2023, Section 2.4). If Frieren were to display the enormous amount of mana she had amassed over her lifetime, others might feel threatened or perceive themselves as inferior. Perhaps they would feel the need to overcompensate, to prove that they are powerful enough. The textbook reports that feelings of inferiority can lead to people making decisions that negatively affect them, such as unnecessary financial purchases (or in Frieren’s world, picking unnecessary fights for status).

Despite hiding her powers, Frieren's self-concept remains strong, as she understands her own abilities regardless of how others perceive her. She, additionally, recognizes which opponents she is capable of defeating – bringing us to another important element of maintaining a positive self-concept: setting realistic goals. Our textbook notes that we can enter negative cycles when we consistently set goals that are too difficult to achieve and repeatedly fail to reach them (Felt & Packer, 2023, Section 2.4). I experienced this, when I consistently took on more intense course schedules than I could handle for several semesters, each time resulting in many failing grades, as I couldn't balance them all. My confidence was shot. I consequently decided to switch to subjects that were easier for me, so that I could build momentum and regain confidence in my abilities. My self-esteem today is much sturdier, as I've consistently managed to reach the goals that I set for myself – the key is that they remain realistic. I think there is something valuable in understanding our own capabilities without feeling the need to constantly demonstrate them to others, as Frieren does when she conceals her mana.

Time: Schemata Can Change

Carstensen et al. (1999) note that our perception of time is malleable, and that our social goals can change when we become aware of time constraints. In other words, what we consider important can change depending on how much time we believe we have. For Frieren, however, it wasn't her own time that she felt the pressure of constraints upon, but rather that of the people around her. The social media fandom surrounding Frieren often compares her relationship with Himmel to that of a human with a dog, particularly in relation to time. We often don't truly realize how short a time we have with our dear pets, and we can feel the weight of it once we begin noticing how quickly they've aged. I felt this with my own dog, as I began to notice the effects of time on her achy joints at only ten years old. I knew that the dog lifespan was short, though I found myself wishing, in hindsight, that I could spend more time with her. My understanding of her lifespan hadn't changed in terms of years, but rather, the significance I attributed to those years had. I think Frieren experienced something similar, as she knew that Himmel would grow old – it wasn't until she lost him that she began to appreciate just how little time they had shared. Her schemata shifted.

Wrapping Things Up…

Throughout Frieren: Beyond Journey's End, Frieren's world view changes, not because the world itself has changed, but rather, the way she interprets her experiences has changed. She revisited memories, considered several interpretations of past interactions, and recognized the importance of relationships that she hadn't appreciated beforehand. Schemata are not fixed, and the meanings we asign to our experiences adjust as we intake new information. Frieren continues to pursue magic and collect grimoires, though she gains a desire to better understand humans as well. Understanding how much time we have with someone is not necessarily the same as appreciating it. Through perception checking, questioning our assumptions, and learning to consider other people's perspectives, we may, in turn, discover new meanings within our interpersonal relationships.
